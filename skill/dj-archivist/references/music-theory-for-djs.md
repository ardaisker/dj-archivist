# Music theory for DJs

Practical theory and audio basics behind mixing and archive decisions. Numbers are rules of
thumb; ears and the dancefloor decide.

## Beats, bars and phrases

- **Beat:** one pulse; in four-on-the-floor music, one kick.
- **Bar:** four beats in 4/4 time. Beat 1 is the downbeat, "the one".
- **Phrase:** a run of bars that makes one musical statement. Dance music is built in 8-bar
  units grouped into 16- and 32-bar sections. Chord progressions usually cycle every 4 or 8
  bars, and arrangement changes (a new element, a breakdown, a drop) land on the first beat of
  a phrase.
- **Arithmetic:** seconds per bar = 240 / BPM; bars in a track = length in seconds × BPM / 240.

| BPM | 1 bar | 8 bars | 16 bars | 32 bars |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 120 | 2.00 s | 16 s | 32 s | 64 s |
| 124 | 1.94 s | 15.5 s | 31 s | 62 s |
| 128 | 1.88 s | 15 s | 30 s | 60 s |
| 140 | 1.71 s | 13.7 s | 27 s | 55 s |
| 150 | 1.60 s | 12.8 s | 26 s | 51 s |
| 174 | 1.38 s | 11 s | 22 s | 44 s |

### Counting and hearing phrases

- Count beats and bars together: "ONE two three four, TWO two three four" up to EIGHT, then
  start again, keeping a tally of eights for 16 and 32. Start on the first downbeat of the
  music, not on silence.
- Boundaries announce themselves: a fill, snare roll, riser, reverse cymbal or dropout in the
  last bar (often the last beat); a crash or impact on the next downbeat; an element entering
  or leaving (hats, bass, vocal); the chord progression starting over.
- Vocals often start a beat or two early (a pickup). The phrase starts on the downbeat, not on
  the first syllable.
- Software helps: bar counters, beat jumps of 16 or 32 beats, phrase analysis (Rekordbox, for
  example, marks sections for many tracks). All of it depends on a correct beatgrid.

### Why mixes land on phrase starts

When phrases line up, both tracks change at the same moments: the incoming intro ends as the
outgoing track reaches a boundary, the bass swap falls on a downbeat and sounds deliberate, and
the drop lands where the floor expects change. A mis-phrased mix makes one track change in the
middle of the other's phrase; dancers feel it as a stumble even when the beats match. Method:
start the incoming track on the downbeat of a phrase in the outgoing track, choosing the phrase
so the incoming intro (16 or 32 bars) ends exactly where the outgoing track reaches its outro
or next section.

Structures are not always regular: an extra bar, a 2-beat pickup, odd-length breakdowns, edits
and live-played records. When something sounds off, recount from the next clear marker and set
memory cues on the real phrase starts.

## Keys and harmonic mixing

A key is a tonal centre plus a scale. Major keys sound brighter, minor keys darker, and every
major key has a relative minor with the same seven notes (C major and A minor). Bassline and
chords reveal a track's key. In an overlap both tracks' notes sound together: compatible keys
share most notes, while incompatible keys create sour intervals, most audible in sustained
chords, pads, vocals and basslines.

### The Camelot wheel

The Camelot wheel, popularized by the Mixed In Key software, numbers the circle of fifths 1 to
12; A is minor, B is major. One step clockwise is up a perfect fifth (seven semitones).
Relative major and minor share a number. Open Key, used by Traktor, is the same wheel with
other labels: m for minor, d for major, counted from A minor (1m) and C major (1d). Camelot to
Open Key: subtract 7 from the number, adding 12 if the result is below 1.

| Camelot | Minor key | Major key | Open Key |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1A / 1B | A♭m (G♯m) | B | 6m / 6d |
| 2A / 2B | E♭m (D♯m) | F♯ (G♭) | 7m / 7d |
| 3A / 3B | B♭m (A♯m) | D♭ (C♯) | 8m / 8d |
| 4A / 4B | Fm | A♭ | 9m / 9d |
| 5A / 5B | Cm | E♭ | 10m / 10d |
| 6A / 6B | Gm | B♭ | 11m / 11d |
| 7A / 7B | Dm | F | 12m / 12d |
| 8A / 8B | Am | C | 1m / 1d |
| 9A / 9B | Em | G | 2m / 2d |
| 10A / 10B | Bm | D | 3m / 3d |
| 11A / 11B | F♯m | A | 4m / 4d |
| 12A / 12B | C♯m (D♭m) | E | 5m / 5d |

Software may write ♯ as "#" and ♭ as "b"; enharmonic names (G♯ and A♭) are the same key.
Applications differ in key notation and several let you switch: pick one notation for your
tags. To place a key by hand, count fifths from the anchors: A, E, B, F♯, C♯, G♯, D♯, B♭, F,
C, G, D run from 8A to 7A; C, G, D, A, E, B, F♯, D♭, A♭, E♭, B♭, F run from 8B to 7B.

### Moves and why they work

| Move | Example | Notes shared (of 7) | Use |
|---|---|---|---|
| Same key | 8A → 8A | 7 | Anything, including long melodic overlaps |
| Relative major or minor | 8A ↔ 8B | 7 | Mood change, darker or brighter, without clash |
| One step | 8A → 9A or 7A | 6 | The standard smooth move; clockwise lifts slightly |
| Diagonal | 8A → 9B, 8B → 7A | 6 | Smooth; mood and key change together |
| Energy boost +2 | 8A → 10A | 5 | Up a whole tone: a clear lift |
| Energy boost +7 | 8A → 3A | 2 | Up a semitone: the pop key-change lift |
| Other jumps | 8A → 11A | 4 or fewer | Cuts, echo-outs and percussive overlaps only |

The other diagonals (8A to 7B, 8B to 9A) also share six notes. Energy boosts lift because the
new key sits higher, but overlapping melodic parts rub, the +7 move especially. Use them at a
drop after a break, after a drums-only moment or as a planned push into a peak, and rarely.
The reverse moves (-2, -7) deflate a room, which is sometimes the point. Shared notes measure
potential, not outcome: the notes actually playing in the overlap decide.

### When to break the rules

- Percussive or atonal material (drum tools, many techno and tech house grooves, beat-only
  intros) mixes over anything.
- A reset lets the ear accept a new key: a breakdown, a drums-only bar, a cut on the downbeat,
  an echo-out.
- Short overlaps forgive more than long ones.
- Intention counts: a deliberate clash can build tension, a classic can justify a jump, and the
  floor is the final judge. Compatible tags do not guarantee a good blend, and incompatible tags
  do not forbid a quick one.

### Key detection pitfalls

- **Relative confusion:** A minor and C major (8A, 8B) share every note, so a detector may pick
  either. Harmless for mixing.
- **Neighbour errors:** results one step off are common, especially with modal music. A track in
  D Dorian uses the notes of C major, so it may be tagged 7A (D minor) or 8A/8B.
- **Tuning:** tracks not tuned to standard pitch (A = 440 Hz), vinyl rips at a slightly wrong
  speed and pitched samples sit between keys; any tag is a compromise and blends sound slightly
  sour.
- **Key changes:** a final chorus lifted a step, or a breakdown in another key; detectors report
  one key per track.
- **Little or no harmony:** drum-led tracks and one-note basslines get near-arbitrary results.
- **Tools disagree:** a key in a filename may come from a different analyser than your
  software. Pick one source of truth and flag disagreements rather than choosing silently.
- **Pitch changes:** with keylock off, about 6 percent faster is a semitone up, +7 on the wheel.

### Why ears beat metadata

Tags are analysis guesses; the overlap you actually play decides. Before a long blend, cue the
incoming track in headphones over the outgoing one and play the sections that will overlap,
not only the intro. Listen for sour, beating intervals, above all in the bass and sustained
notes. If it clashes, blend over drums, shorten the overlap or pick another track. Write what
you hear back into the archive (a corrected key, or a note such as "sounds 7A") so tags
improve over time.

## Tempo, pitch and keylock

- **Ranges:** club players commonly offer ±6, ±10 and ±16 percent plus a wide setting; a
  narrower range gives finer control.
- **Keylock off** (vinyl behaviour): speed and pitch move together. One semitone is about +5.9
  or -5.6 percent; 1 percent is about 17 cents (hundredths of a semitone). At ±3 percent a
  track is roughly half a semitone out, midway between two keys, the worst case for a harmonic
  overlap. Recheck harmony after changes beyond 2 to 3 percent.
- **Keylock on** (Master Tempo in Rekordbox and on Pioneer DJ players, Keylock in Serato and
  Traktor): time-stretching holds the pitch. Artifacts grow with the stretch: softer, smeared
  transients, phasey or warbling sustained notes, grainy or metallic vocals. A few percent is
  usually inaudible on a club system; beyond roughly 6 to 8 percent artifacts become easier to
  hear, especially on vocals, pianos and pads. Algorithms differ between applications and
  players.
- **Key shift:** transposing by a semitone moves a track 7 steps on the wheel; the same
  artifacts apply, so keep shifts small.
- **Feel:** even without artifacts, tempo changes character. A deep house track pushed from 120
  to 128 BPM (+6.7 percent) sounds rushed; tech house dragged from 128 to 120 sounds heavy. Move
  a set's tempo a BPM or two per track and save big jumps for cuts, breakdowns and genre
  changes.

### Half and double time

Tempos in a 2:1 ratio lock: each beat of the slower track falls on every other beat of the
faster one.

| Slow | Fast | Typical pairing |
|---|---|---|
| 70 | 140 | half-time hip-hop and trap with dubstep and grime |
| 85-87 | 170-174 | hip-hop and halftime with drum and bass |
| 80 | 160 | hip-hop with footwork and juke |

Dubstep at 140 feels like 70, and drum and bass at 174 is often felt at 87, which is why these
pairs sound natural. Analysis sometimes reports half or double tempo; most applications let you
set the BPM range used for analysis, so fix wrong readings instead of living with them. Other
ways across a tempo gap: a cut on a drop, an echo-out and a fresh start, a beatless breakdown or
intro, or a DJ-pool transition edit.

Live-played music (disco, funk, soul, rock) drifts. Use a variable-tempo analysis mode where
available (Rekordbox has one) or place grid markers by hand, and be ready to beatmatch by ear.

## Energy and set programming

Energy is perceived intensity: tempo, kick and bass weight, density, brightness, tension and
release, vocals, familiarity. It is relative to the room and the hour. Rate it in the archive
(1 to 10 in a tag or comment works) and tag each track's role: warm-up, builder, peak, release,
closer.

| Shape | What it does | Suits |
|---|---|---|
| Arc | Warm-up, build, peak, release | Club and festival slots with a story |
| Waves or hump | Several peaks and valleys, or one apex around 60 percent through | Long sets; valleys make peaks bigger |
| Plateau | Steady energy in a narrow BPM band; variety from texture and harmony | Focus sets, hypnotic techno, much drum and bass |
| Staircase | Step up, hold, step up | Warm-ups and handovers |

- **Warm-up:** build the room, not your reputation: lower tempo and energy, few anthems or big
  drops, grooves that draw people in. Leave the next DJ room to climb and hand over at a level
  they can build on.
- **Peak time:** the strongest material, still with contrast; a breakdown, a vocal or a classic
  every so often stops the floor going numb.
- **Closing:** a final run of anthems or a controlled wind-down (warmer, slower, emotional),
  depending on the venue; make the last track sound like an ending.
- **Vocal density:** vocals demand attention. Alternate vocal and instrumental tracks, never
  overlap two vocals, save heavy vocals for moments that deserve focus (after a breakdown, as
  release after an apex), and use dubs and instrumentals as connective tissue.
- **Harmony as energy:** clockwise steps lift gently, minor to relative major brightens, and
  energy boosts are planned events.
- **Plan, then react:** plan the shape and keep several candidates per slot rather than a fixed
  tracklist; the room overrides the plan.

## Frequencies and EQ

| Band | Approx. range | What lives there | DJ notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sub | 20-60 Hz | kick fundamentals, sub bass | Felt more than heard; the main headroom risk |
| Bass | 60-250 Hz | kick body, basslines | Where two basslines fight |
| Low mids | 250-500 Hz | bass and pad body, warmth | Pile-ups sound boomy and blurred |
| Mids | 500 Hz-2 kHz | vocals, leads, chords, snare body | Where melodies and vocals clash |
| Upper mids | 2-6 kHz | presence, attack, consonants | Harshness and ear fatigue |
| Highs | 6-20 kHz | hi-hats, cymbals, air, sibilance | Sparkle; lossy files lose detail here first |

Ranges overlap and vary by source. Mixer EQ bands split the spectrum at model-specific points;
isolator-style EQs can remove a band completely, while conventional EQs cut by a limited
amount. Know which you have.

- **Cut, don't boost.** Boosting eats headroom and pushes meters into the red; cutting the
  other track gets the same balance.
- **The bass swap:** bring the incoming track in with its low band fully cut (mids often
  slightly down) and blend highs and mids over the phrase. On the downbeat of the next phrase,
  cut the outgoing low and open the incoming low in one move, then fade the rest of the outgoing
  track.
- **Why never two basslines at once:**
  - Harmony: low notes close together beat against each other, and two independent basslines
    rarely agree even in compatible keys.
  - Physics: two kicks and basslines sum unpredictably. In phase they add a lot of level;
    slightly out of phase they cancel; the low end booms or thins.
  - Headroom: the low end carries most of the energy. Doubling it drives the master and the
    venue limiter, which then squashes everything.
  - Clarity: bass masks everything above it.
  - Exception: a brief overlap where one track has almost no low end (a drum tool, a
    high-passed layer).
- **Mids:** two vocals or leads cannot share the band; drop the outgoing mids when a vocal
  arrives.
- **Highs:** layered hats add drive and hide a transition, but mismatched swing sounds messy:
  cut the highs on one track.
- **Filters:** a high-pass filter removes lows (thin the outgoing track, or bring the incoming
  in as a shadow); a low-pass filter removes highs (push a track back, build tension).
  Resonance adds a peak at the cutoff that can turn harsh on big systems. Filters are
  transition tools: return to centre on a phrase boundary and don't leave a track filtered for
  long.

## Gain staging and loudness

Signal chain: file → trim (gain) → EQ → channel fader → crossfader → master → booth and main
system, which usually has its own limiter.

- **Trim sets level; faders mix.** Set each trim so the channel meter peaks at the same point
  (typically the top of the green or the first amber segments), with the fader fully up once
  the track is fully in. Mixing with faders at 70 percent leaves the next track nowhere to go.
- **Headroom** is the space between normal peaks and clipping. Digital audio clips hard at 0
  dBFS, analogue stages distort more gently, and venue systems limit when driven. Red lights
  mean distortion or limiting: louder in the booth, worse on the floor. If the master runs hot,
  lower the trims or the master, not only the booth.
- **Booth:** set the booth monitor for yourself, not the room; a quiet booth tempts you to push
  the master. Wear ear protection.
- **Perceived loudness is not peak level.** Two tracks can peak identically and differ by
  several dB in loudness because of compression, limiting and frequency balance: bright,
  mid-heavy tracks sound louder, while sub-heavy tracks move meters without sounding loud.
- **LUFS** (loudness units relative to full scale, defined in ITU-R BS.1770 and used by EBU
  R128) weight frequencies roughly as the ear does. Integrated LUFS covers a whole track,
  short-term about 3 seconds, momentary 400 ms. Streaming services normalize playback (commonly
  to around -14 LUFS); DJ software plays files as they are unless auto gain is on. Many modern
  club masters measure in the single-digit negatives (roughly -9 to -5 LUFS integrated), while
  older releases, vinyl rips and dynamic productions are often several dB quieter.
- **Matching:** auto gain (Rekordbox, Serato and Traktor all offer it) is a starting point;
  then adjust by ear and meter, comparing the sections that will actually play together (drop
  against drop, not intro against drop).
- **Level-match before judging.** When comparing two versions of a file, the louder one always
  seems better at first.

## File quality

| Format | Type | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| WAV | Lossless, uncompressed | Plays everywhere; tag support is limited and inconsistent between applications |
| AIFF | Lossless, uncompressed | Same audio as WAV with reliable tags; the safe lossless choice for USB and older players |
| FLAC | Lossless, compressed | Smaller files, good tags; older club players cannot read it |
| ALAC | Lossless, compressed (Apple) | Missing from Traktor's supported list; older players cannot read it |
| MP3 320 kbps CBR | Lossy | Long-standing club standard; transparent for most material |
| MP3 VBR (such as V0) | Lossy | Similar quality, but seeking and cue positions have historically been less reliable on some players and across applications; many DJs standardize on CBR |
| AAC/M4A 256 kbps | Lossy | Common store format; good quality |
| MP3 at 128-192 kbps | Lossy | Audible on big systems (swishy cymbals, smeared transients); replace |

Support in the major applications (check current documentation, since details change):

| Application | MP3 | AAC/M4A | WAV | AIFF | FLAC | ALAC | Ogg Vorbis |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Rekordbox | yes | yes | yes | yes | yes | yes | no |
| Serato DJ Pro | yes | yes | yes | yes | yes | yes | yes |
| Traktor Pro | yes | yes | yes | yes | yes | no | yes |

Some support depends on the operating system (Serato's ALAC and raw AAC files, for example),
and DRM-protected files play in none of them. Club players lag behind software: the CDJ-2000
and CDJ-2000NXS generation reads MP3, AAC, WAV and AIFF but not FLAC or ALAC; later models such
as the CDJ-2000NXS2 and CDJ-3000 added both, along with files up to 96 kHz/24-bit. When the
booth is unknown, bring AIFF or MP3 320.

**Sample rate and bit depth:** 44.1 kHz/16-bit (CD standard) is enough for club playback;
48 kHz is common from video workflows; 24-bit and higher sample rates help production, not
playback. High-resolution files are larger, some older players refuse them, and the software
resamples to the audio interface's rate anyway.

### Spotting transcodes and fake quality

A transcode is a lossy file re-encoded at a higher bitrate (a 128 kbps MP3 saved as 320); fake
lossless is a lossy file decoded into WAV, AIFF or FLAC; upsampling raises sample rate or bit
depth without adding content. DJs often call all three "upsampled". None restores what the
first encode removed.

Open the file in a spectrogram (Spek, Audacity's spectrogram view or similar). MP3 encoders
low-pass the audio, harder at lower bitrates:

| Source | Typical top of the spectrum |
|---|---|
| MP3 128 kbps | about 16 kHz (older encoders) to 17 kHz (LAME) |
| MP3 192 kbps | about 18.5-19 kHz |
| MP3 320 kbps | about 19.5-20.5 kHz |
| Lossless from CD | up to about 22 kHz, if the master has content there |

- A "320" or lossless file with a hard, flat ceiling at 16-17 kHz came from a 128 kbps source.
- A lossless file with a ceiling near 20 kHz was probably made from a high-bitrate MP3.
- Patchy, blocky energy in the top band that switches on and off with the music is a lossy
  fingerprint.
- A 96 kHz file with nothing above about 22 kHz (or 24 kHz) was upsampled from 44.1 kHz (or
  48 kHz).
- 192 against 320 is hard to tell by eye; the ceilings are close.
- Caveats: some masters are low-passed on purpose, old recordings and vinyl rips roll off
  naturally (a gentle slope, not a wall), and some music is simply dark. A spectrogram is
  evidence, not proof: confirm on good headphones, listening to cymbals and reverb tails for
  swishy, watery or metallic artifacts.
- Never transcode lossy to lossy (MP3 to AAC, or MP3 to another MP3 bitrate), and never expect
  a lossy-to-lossless conversion to improve anything. Lossless to lossless (AIFF, FLAC, WAV) is
  safe for the audio but can drop tags.

## Library hygiene

- **Tags:** artist (featured artists written one consistent way), title with the version in
  parentheses ("Title (Extended Mix)"), remixer, label, year, genre from your own vocabulary,
  BPM, key in one notation, a comment for energy, role and notes, embedded artwork. Strip shop
  spam and URLs.
- **File names:** one pattern everywhere, such as "Artist - Title (Mix).ext".
- **One file per track:** each version is its own track, but each track exists once.
  Duplicates split cue points, play history and playlist membership; keep the best copy and
  point every playlist at it.
- **Stable paths:** move and rename files through the DJ software, or relocate them
  afterwards; moving files behind its back creates missing-file errors.
- **Analysis and beatgrids:** analyse before the gig, not at it. Check the first downbeat marker
  and the grid near the end (drift shows late), fix half or double BPM readings, and use
  variable grids for live-played music. A wrong grid breaks sync, quantized cues, loops and
  beat jumps.
- **Cue points:** one scheme and one colour code, for example a memory cue on the first downbeat
  and hot cues at the mix-in point, first drop, breakdown and mix-out point. Cues and grids live
  mainly in the software's database, so back that up too. After moving a library between
  applications, spot-check cues: some MP3s decode with a small offset in different software.
- **Intake routine:** new file → format and quality check (spectrogram when in doubt) → tags →
  analysis → grid check → cues → crate. Unverified files stay out of gig playlists.
- **Backups:** follow 3-2-1: three copies, on two kinds of media, one off-site. Back up the
  library database with the music (Rekordbox has a built-in library backup, Serato keeps its
  data in _Serato_ folders, Traktor keeps its collection in collection.nml). Carry two USB
  drives exported from the same library, and test a restore now and then.

## Common mistakes checklist

- [ ] Two basslines (or two kicks' low ends) at once.
- [ ] Mixing across phrases, so the incoming drop lands mid-phrase.
- [ ] Trusting key and BPM tags without listening; half or double BPM readings left unfixed.
- [ ] Keylock off while pushing tempo (keys drift apart), or on for big jumps (artifacts).
- [ ] Master or channels in the red; mixing on the channel fader instead of setting trims.
- [ ] Boosting EQ instead of cutting; filters left on after the transition.
- [ ] Two vocals at once, or a long run of vocal-heavy tracks.
- [ ] Peak energy without release.
- [ ] Low-bitrate, transcoded or fake lossless files in gig playlists.
- [ ] Radio edits or "(Mixed)" compilation versions where a club version exists.
- [ ] Duplicate files with different cues; files moved outside the DJ software.
- [ ] Unchecked beatgrids on live-played or older records.
- [ ] Formats the venue's players cannot read.
- [ ] One USB drive, and no backup of the music and library database.
